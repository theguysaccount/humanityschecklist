The world is not short of wealth. Global output has grown many times over in the last century. Yet the share of that wealth reaching most people remains small, and that gap shapes every other problem humanity faces.

## How wealth is distributed

The World Inequality Report 2022 estimated that the richest 10 percent of the world's population own about 76 percent of all wealth, while the poorest half own about 2 percent. At the very top, Forbes counted more than 2,700 billionaires in 2024, with combined fortunes above $14 trillion.

Numbers like these are often argued about at the margins, but the overall picture is not in dispute. A small share of people hold most of the world's wealth.

## Why it is the key problem

Wealth distribution is not one problem among many. It decides who can act on all the others.

- **Poverty, hunger and disease** follow directly from who has resources.
- **Education and opportunity** follow from who can afford them.
- **Political power** follows money, which shapes which problems get addressed at all.
- **Gatekeeping** follows capital: the people who fund what gets built decide what the future looks like.

When wealth pools in a few places, the decisions about humanity's future pool there too. That is why it sits so close to the center of so many crises.

## Why the usual fixes struggle

The usual responses are taxation, regulation and aid. These debates matter, and they will continue. But they share a weakness: they push against where money wants to go, so they are politically contested, slow to pass and easy to undo.

Meanwhile, surplus wealth keeps flowing to the same place it always has: status.

## Where surplus wealth goes

Once basic needs are met, much of what people spend goes to status. More than $300 billion a year flows into designer goods. For a stretch, the richest person on Earth built his fortune selling them. As the economist Thorstein Veblen pointed out in 1899, a great deal of spending is about displaying wealth rather than using it.

This is the key. **Wealth follows status.** What a culture admires is where its surplus money goes. If the most admired thing is visible spending on yourself, wealth concentrates. If the most admired thing is visible contribution to others, wealth moves.

## Redefining status

That is the idea behind Redefine Status. In "How We Can Correct Mankind's Leaderboard" at [redefinestatus.com](https://redefinestatus.com), it argues that status should reward positive-sum behavior, and that our status symbols, starting with what we wear, should show what we have done for the world rather than how much we can waste. It calls this status engineering. Its example is pointed: the price of a single pair of designer shoes could build a small permanent home for a family.

Redefine Status goes deeper into the fix. Humanity's Checklist puts it in context as one of six sources upstream of every world problem, with **charity designer goods** as its beachhead: [pieces where the coolest thing you can wear shows how much you gave](/blog/giving-as-the-new-status-symbol/).

## Why this works with human nature

Redistribution by force asks people to give up what they want. Redefining status changes what they want. People will keep seeking status; that drive is not going away. The question is only what earns it.

There is precedent. The Giving Pledge, launched in 2010 by Bill Gates and Warren Buffett, asks the world's wealthiest people to publicly commit most of their wealth to philanthropy. Part of its power is social: a public pledge makes giving visible and admired among peers. Charity designer goods aim to do the same thing for everyone, not just billionaires.

## The domino effect

Change status, and the money starts to move without a single law passing. That money funds the rest of the checklist: better feeds, better coordination, better visions of the future. It changes the story a culture tells about success. And it shifts the distribution of wealth from the bottom up, one admired choice at a time.

The world does not lack wealth. It lacks a culture that admires putting it to work for everyone. That is a problem we can solve, and [the cost to solve it is close to zero](/blog/the-cost-to-save-humanity-is-zero/).
