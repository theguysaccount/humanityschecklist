Almost every large human activity has a science with a name. Crime has criminology. Disease has epidemiology. Living systems have ecology. Money has economics. Even bells have campanology.

Charity, one of the oldest and largest things humans do, has no widely used name for its science.

## How big charity is

Giving is not a niche activity.

- **It is ancient.** Charity is built into most of the world's major traditions: zakat is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, tzedakah is a central duty in Judaism, dana is a core practice in Buddhism and Hinduism, and Christianity puts charity among the highest virtues.
- **It is enormous.** Americans alone give more than $500 billion a year.
- **It is a workforce.** The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics has counted about 12.5 million people working for nonprofits, roughly one in ten private-sector jobs.

An activity that old and that large would normally have its own field, its own vocabulary and its own core questions.

## What exists instead

Giving is studied, but in pieces. Economists study why people donate. Psychologists study what moves them. Sociologists study nonprofits as organizations. Universities run programs in philanthropic studies and nonprofit management. The effective altruism movement compares causes by cost-effectiveness.

Each of these does valuable work. But they sit in different departments, use different words and ask different questions. There is no single name that gathers them into one science with a shared goal: understanding what charity actually changes, and how to make it change more.

## Why names matter

Names do not just label fields; they help create them. When Ernst Haeckel coined the word **ecology** in 1866, he gave a scattered set of observations about organisms and their surroundings a single identity, and a discipline grew around it. When Raffaele Garofalo titled his 1885 book *Criminologia*, he helped turn the study of crime into a field with its own questions.

Without a name, a subject stays fragmented. Findings in one corner never reach the others. Practitioners have no shared map. And the most important questions, the ones that cross departmental lines, go unasked.

## The cost of the missing science

Because charity has no science of its own, it is mostly guided by instinct, habit and attention. People give to the faces they see and the stories that move them. Organizations are rewarded for outputs they can count this year. Disasters with heavy news coverage attract more money than slow, structural problems.

The result is the pattern described in [why charity hasn't solved the world's problems](/blog/why-charity-hasnt-solved-world-problems/): enormous generosity aimed mostly at symptoms, while the systems that produce the need keep running.

## Charitology

**Charitology** is a name for the missing science: the study of charity as a system. It asks:

- What does a gift actually change, and for how long?
- Which systems does giving strengthen or weaken?
- Why do people give the way they do, and what would redirect them?
- How should givers coordinate so their gifts add up?
- How does status shape where generosity goes?

For a fuller introduction, read [What Is Charitology?](/blog/what-is-charitology/) and visit [charitology.com](https://charitology.com).

## From the science to the practice

A science leads to a practice. The practice that follows from charitology is [systemic altruism](/blog/systemic-altruism/): aiming generosity at the sources that keep producing need. And its first application is Humanity's Checklist, which names six of those sources and asks what would happen if giving finally aimed at them.

It is strange that the science of charity never got a name. Giving it one might be the first step toward charity that solves problems instead of just treating them.
