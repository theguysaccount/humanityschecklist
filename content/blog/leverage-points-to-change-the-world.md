"Give me a lever long enough and a place to stand, and I will move the world." The line is attributed to Archimedes, and it captures the dream of anyone who wants to change things: find the spot where a small push makes a big difference.

The systems thinker Donella Meadows spent her career on that question. In her 1999 essay "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System," she listed twelve places to push on a complex system, ranked from least to most effective.

## Meadows' list, from weakest to strongest

1. Constants, parameters and numbers, such as subsidies, taxes and standards.
2. The sizes of buffers and stocks relative to their flows.
3. The structure of material stocks and flows.
4. The lengths of delays relative to how fast the system changes.
5. The strength of negative feedback loops.
6. The gain around positive feedback loops.
7. The structure of information flows: who has access to what information.
8. The rules of the system: incentives, punishments and constraints.
9. The power to add, change or self-organize system structure.
10. The goals of the system.
11. The mindset or paradigm out of which the system arises.
12. The power to transcend paradigms.

Meadows numbered her list the other way, from 12 down to 1, so that the strongest point came last. The order is the same.

Her striking observation was that people usually push on the weakest points. Most debates are about numbers: how much funding, what tax rate, which target. Those matter, but they rarely change how a system behaves. The deep changes come from information, rules, goals and, above all, the paradigm.

## Where charity usually pushes

Most charity works at the bottom of the list. It adjusts flows: more meals delivered, more beds funded, more money moved from one place to another. That is valuable, and it is also why the same needs reappear every year. The system that produces them has not changed.

## Where Humanity's Checklist pushes

The checklist is ordered as a triangle, and the order follows the same logic as Meadows' list. Read loosely against her points:

- **Attention** is about the structure of information flows: what billions of people see each day.
- **Status** is about incentives: what a culture rewards.
- **Gatekeepers** are about the power to self-organize: who is allowed to build new structures.
- **Vision** is about the goals of the system: what humanity is trying to reach.
- **Story** is the paradigm itself: what humanity believes it is.
- **Coordination** runs through all of them, because every leverage point needs people to act together.

The higher an item sits on the triangle, the more of everything below it changes. That is why the story we tell ourselves sits at the very top.

## Why the high points are neglected

If high leverage points are so powerful, why does so little money go to them? Because they are hard to measure, slow to show results and impossible to credit to a single donor. A meal served is easy to count. A shifted paradigm is not.

That is the gap the checklist is built for. It names six specific high-leverage sources, pairs each with a concrete project and proposes early indicators, so that work at the top of Meadows' list can be planned, funded and checked like any other work.

## Finding your own leverage

You can apply the same test to anything you support. Ask:

- Is this changing a number, or changing a rule, a goal or a belief?
- Who sees different information because of this?
- What does this make people want, admire or expect?
- If it works, how many other problems get easier?

The answers point toward the places where a small push moves the most.
