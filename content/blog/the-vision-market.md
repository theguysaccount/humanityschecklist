Ask a room of people to describe how the world might end and you will get dozens of detailed answers: climate collapse, pandemics, rogue AI, nuclear war. Ask them to describe a future they would actually want to live in, in the same detail, and the room goes quiet.

That silence is a problem. We cannot build what we cannot see.

## The missing picture

In the 1950s the Dutch sociologist Fred Polak studied how cultures imagine the future. In *The Image of the Future*, he argued that societies with vivid, hopeful pictures of where they were headed tended to thrive, and that cultures lose their energy when those images fade.

Today our most vivid images are of collapse. Fear holds attention, so films, news and feeds lean toward dystopia. The result is a culture that can describe many ways the world ends and very few ways it gets better. Without a shared, compelling picture, there is nothing for people to coordinate around.

## What a vision market is

The Vision Market is Humanity's Checklist's beachhead for this problem: a market for visions of the future.

- **Anyone can create a vision.** A picture of a city, an industry, a school, a way of life, or the whole planet a decade from now.
- **Visions are made visible.** Images, stories, models and maps, so people can see them rather than just read about them.
- **People back the ones they believe in.** With attention, skills, time and money.
- **The strongest visions attract builders.** Over time, a shared picture becomes a shared plan.

## Vision markets vs. prediction markets

Prediction markets already exist. The Iowa Electronic Markets have let people trade on election outcomes since 1988, and forecasting research such as Philip Tetlock's work on superforecasters has shown that crowds can predict events surprisingly well.

A vision market asks a different question. A prediction market asks **what will happen**. A vision market asks **what should happen, and who will help make it happen**. One measures belief about the future. The other gathers commitment to it.

There are also precedents for backing visions directly. Crowdfunding platforms such as Kickstarter, launched in 2009, let people fund projects that do not exist yet. Prize challenges such as the Ansari X Prize, won in 2004 by the privately built SpaceShipOne, showed how a clearly stated goal can pull talent and money toward it. The Vision Market applies the same logic to whole futures.

## Why vision sits near the top

On the triangle, vision sits just below the story we tell ourselves, beside coordination. It is the goal of the system, in the language of systems thinker Donella Meadows, and goals are among the strongest places to intervene.

A shared vision does three things at once:

1. **It gives coordination a destination.** The Social Router can connect people, but a vision tells them what to build together.
2. **It makes the new story concrete.** "Every problem is solvable" becomes a picture you can point at.
3. **It hands builders a blueprint.** The Co-Creator Portal needs ideas worth building; the Vision Market supplies them.

## Why now

For most of history, previewing a future meant writing a novel or building an expensive model. Today, tools for image, video and 3D generation let anyone visualize a future in minutes. The cost of showing what a better world looks like has collapsed. What is missing is a place where those pictures can be compared, refined and backed.

## What you can do now

- Describe, in detail, one future you would want to live in, and share it.
- Look for the people already building pieces of it.
- Back visions with more than likes: with skills, time or money.

Dystopias tell us what to fear. A vision market tells us what to build.
