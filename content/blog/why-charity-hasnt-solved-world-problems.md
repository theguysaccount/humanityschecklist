Every year, hundreds of billions of dollars go to charity. In the United States alone, giving by individuals, foundations, bequests and companies tops $500 billion a year. So why does the list of world problems never seem to get shorter?

It is not because charity fails. The world has made real progress: extreme poverty and child mortality have fallen sharply over the past few decades, and charity played a part alongside economic growth, science and public health. The question is why so many problems keep coming back after we pay to fix them.

## 1. Charity mostly treats symptoms

Most giving goes to the problem in front of us: a meal, a shelter bed, a vaccine, a disaster appeal. That work matters, and it saves lives. But treating a case does not stop the process that creates the next one. Budgets aimed at symptoms never finish, because new cases keep arriving.

A doctor who only ever treats fevers will be busy forever. At some point, someone has to treat the infection.

## 2. Problems are connected and budgets are not

Climate stress drives migration. Migration strains politics. Political conflict drives hunger. Each problem has its own charities, conferences and funding streams, and almost nobody is paid to work on the links between them. A donor who funds one silo cannot see how the next silo is feeding it.

## 3. What gets measured gets funded

Funders understandably want to know what their money achieved. That pushes giving toward outcomes that can be counted within a year, and away from work on causes, which is slower and harder to attribute. The result is a sector that is very good at relief and chronically underfunded for prevention.

## 4. Giving follows attention

Donations rise after disasters that dominate the news and fall when the cameras leave. Slow, structural problems rarely make headlines, so they rarely get the money. Our collective attention is steered by feeds tuned for alarm, which means charity often follows the most shocking story rather than the most important cause.

## 5. Status points the wrong way

Far more money flows into status than into solutions. More than $300 billion a year goes to designer goods. If status were attached to giving instead of spending, a fraction of that flow would dwarf most philanthropy budgets.

## The world does not have a charity problem

Put those five reasons together and a pattern appears. The world does not have a charity problem. It has a **coordination**, **vision** and **status** problem. The resources exist. What is missing is a way to aim them at the sources that keep generating the need.

That is what Humanity's Checklist tries to do. It names six sources upstream of every world problem:

1. The story humanity tells itself.
2. How people find each other and coordinate.
3. Whether we can picture a shared future.
4. What our social feeds reward.
5. What earns status.
6. Who gets to build.

Each comes with one focused project, called a beachhead, designed to flip that source from producing harm to producing repair.

## What giving to causes looks like

Giving to causes does not mean abandoning relief. People need help today. It means adding a second question to every gift: does this change the system that created the need?

- A food bank feeds people this week. A tool that connects farmers, distributors and local organizers changes who goes hungry next year.
- A grant to fight one piece of misinformation helps once. Changing what feeds reward helps with every piece.
- A gala raises money for one season. Making generosity the status symbol raises money every season.

No amount of funding for Band-Aids will ever heal the core. Funding the core might be cheaper than anyone expects.
