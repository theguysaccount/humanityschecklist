There are three ways to try to make the world better.

**Charity** helps the person in front of you. **Effective altruism** asks which help does the most good per dollar. **Systemic altruism** asks what keeps producing the need for help, and changes that.

Systemic altruism is the framework behind Humanity's Checklist. It is how a single question, "why do the same problems keep coming back?", turned into six sources, six beachheads and one domino effect.

## The core idea

Most problems are not one-off events. They are outputs of systems that keep running. A system that produces poverty, loneliness or distrust will keep producing them no matter how many individual cases are treated.

Systemic altruism aims at the system. Its unit of analysis is not the person helped or the dollar spent but the **source**: a shared system that sits upstream of many problems at once. Its measure is not cost per outcome but **leverage**: how many problems one change reaches.

## Charity, effective altruism and systemic altruism

| | Charity | Effective altruism | Systemic altruism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Question | Who needs help now? | Which help does the most good per dollar? | What keeps producing the need? |
| Target | Individual cases | Proven interventions | Sources shared by many problems |
| Measure | Need met | Cost per outcome | Leverage and compounding |
| Strength | Immediate relief | Rigor and evidence | Reaches problems at their root |
| Blind spot | The need returns | What cannot be measured | Hard to attribute results |

None of these replaces the others. People need relief today, and evidence matters. Systemic altruism adds the layer that the other two tend to miss. For a fuller comparison, read [Effective Altruism vs. Humanity's Checklist](/blog/effective-altruism-vs-humanitys-checklist/).

## The framework, step by step

### 1. Map the symptoms

List the problems you care about without filtering: climate change, war, poverty, hunger, disease, loneliness, polarization, misinformation. This is the base of the triangle.

### 2. Find the shared sources

Look for what sits beneath many of them at once. A real source passes five tests: it is **shared** across unrelated problems, **upstream** of them, has **reach** into daily life, is **flippable** rather than removable, and is **buildable** by a small team. See [the root causes of the world's problems](/blog/root-causes-of-world-problems/).

### 3. Rank by leverage

Order the sources by how much of the system they change. Donella Meadows' [leverage points](/blog/leverage-points-to-change-the-world/) are the guide: changing a paradigm beats changing a goal, which beats changing a rule, which beats changing a number. That is why the story sits at the top of the triangle.

### 4. Find the flip

Sources cannot be deleted. People will always tell stories, seek status and pay attention to something. The move is to redirect the same energy toward repair. Status stays, but it rewards giving. Feeds stay, but they rank for value.

### 5. Choose a beachhead

Pick one focused, buildable project per source. A good beachhead is small to start, compounds as it grows, sustains itself through incentives people already have, is open for others to build on, and has an early signal that shows whether it works.

### 6. Measure leading indicators

Systemic change is slow, so watch the signals that move first: introductions that become collaborations, people who expect a better future, money given through status. Treat the problems at the base as lagging indicators.

### 7. Iterate in the open

Publish the map, the reasoning and the results. Let others challenge and improve them. Humanity's Checklist is versioned like an aviation checklist and open source for exactly this reason.

## The principles underneath

Four ideas run through the whole framework.

- **Dominoes.** Sources reinforce each other, so fixing one helps the others.
- **Fractals.** The same pattern holds for a person, a city and a species.
- **Redirection over new money.** The resources already exist; [the cost to save humanity is zero](/blog/the-cost-to-save-humanity-is-zero/) once existing flows are pointed somewhere better.
- **Protopia.** The goal is a world a little better every year, not a perfect one.

## Study, practice, application

Systemic altruism sits in a simple chain. [Charitology](/blog/charitology-the-missing-science-of-charity/) is the study of charity: what giving changes and why. Systemic altruism is the practice that follows from that study. Humanity's Checklist is its first application: six sources, six beachheads, and the question of whether $5 million spent at the source could do more than budgets far larger spent on symptoms.

The only effective altruism is systemic.
