## Abstract

Most efforts to improve the world work on symptoms: a disease, a famine, a war, a polluted river. That work saves lives and should continue. But the same problems keep coming back, because the systems that produce them are left untouched.

Humanity's Checklist proposes a different unit of analysis. It treats humanity as one collective superorganism and world problems as symptoms of a few systems inside it. It names six of those systems, which it calls sources, and orders them by leverage in a triangle. The story we tell ourselves sits at the top. Coordination and vision sit beneath it. Attention, status and gatekeeping sit beneath those. Every world problem sits at the base.

For each source, the checklist proposes one beachhead: a focused, buildable project that flips the source from producing harm to producing repair. It then asks a deliberately provocative question. What if $5 million, spent on those six beachheads, did more than budgets thousands of times larger spent on symptoms?

This paper sets out the philosophy behind that question, the method used to choose sources and judge beachheads, how progress could be measured, and the strongest objections to the whole approach.

## 1. The problem with solving problems

Hundreds of billions of dollars go to charity every year. In the United States alone, individuals, foundations, bequests and companies give more than $500 billion a year. Governments and international agencies add far more. Much of this money does real good, and the world has made real progress: extreme poverty and child mortality have fallen sharply over the past few decades.

Yet the list of world problems does not get shorter. Climate change, conflict, hunger, loneliness, polarization and distrust all persist, and several are growing. Three features of the way we work on them help explain why.

- **Symptoms regenerate.** Treating a case does not stop the process that creates the next one. Budgets aimed at symptoms have no natural end, because the flow of new cases continues.
- **Problems are linked, budgets are not.** Climate stress feeds migration, migration feeds political conflict, conflict feeds hunger. Each problem has its own organizations, conferences and funding streams, and almost nobody owns the connections between them.
- **What gets measured gets funded.** Funders reward outcomes that can be counted this year. Work on causes is slower, more diffuse and harder to attribute, so it is chronically underfunded even when it might matter most.

A medical analogy makes the point. A doctor who treats only the fever, and never the infection, will be busy forever. Putting Band-Aids on an infection is not a strategy. The question this paper asks is whether there is a level at which a single intervention reaches many problems at once.

## 2. Philosophy

### 2.1 Humanity as a superorganism

Eight billion people, linked by trade, media, culture and the internet, behave in some ways like one body. Information moves through it like signals through a nervous system. Rewards shape what it does. It imagines futures and acts toward them. It has a picture of itself.

The checklist uses this as a working model, not a claim about biology. People are not cells, and the diversity and freedom of individuals are part of what makes the whole worth healing. But the model is useful because it points at organs rather than symptoms:

- **The self-image:** the story humanity tells about itself.
- **The synapses:** how people find each other and coordinate.
- **The imagination:** whether we can picture a shared future.
- **The nervous system:** social media and the feeds that carry attention.
- **The reward system:** status, and what earns it.
- **The hands:** who gets to build things, and who decides.

When these organs malfunction, symptoms appear everywhere downstream. When they work, the organism can heal itself.

### 2.2 Sources and symptoms

A **symptom** is a problem people experience directly: hunger, a flooded town, a lonely teenager. A **source** is a system that, in its current form, keeps producing many symptoms at once.

The test of a source is that it is shared. The same source shows up beneath problems that look unrelated. A culture that expects decline undermines climate action, public health and civic life alike. Feeds tuned for outrage worsen polarization, misinformation, anxiety and distrust simultaneously. When one change would move many problems, you have found a source.

### 2.3 Every problem is a coordination problem

Most large problems are not mainly shortages of knowledge or money. They are failures to get people who already have both to act together. Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968) described how shared resources get depleted when each person acts alone. Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) explained why large groups struggle to pursue even goals that all their members share. Thomas Schelling's work on focal points (1960) showed that people coordinate far more easily when they share a visible reference point. Writers on multipolar traps, sometimes called Moloch after Scott Alexander's 2014 essay, describe how competition locks everyone into outcomes nobody wants.

The common thread is that solutions often exist and fail to assemble. That is why coordination sits near the top of the triangle, and why its beachhead is a system for routing people to each other.

### 2.4 Leverage and the order of the triangle

The systems thinker Donella Meadows ranked twelve places to intervene in a system, from least to most effective. At the bottom are constants and parameters: the numbers, such as budgets, subsidies and standards. Near the top are the goals of the system and, above them, the mindset or paradigm from which the system arises.

Most charity and policy work adjusts parameters: more money here, a new target there. The triangle is ordered to push higher. Read loosely against Meadows' list, attention concerns the structure of information flows, status concerns incentives, open creation concerns the power of a system to reorganize itself, a shared vision concerns the system's goals, and the story is the paradigm itself. Coordination runs through all of them. The higher an item sits, the more of everything else it changes.

### 2.5 The flip

None of the six sources can be removed. People will always tell stories, seek status, pay attention to something and want to build. The aim is not to suppress these drives but to redirect them. The same energy, pointed somewhere new, becomes the cure.

This is why each item on the checklist is phrased as a flip. Status seeking stays, but the coolest thing you can wear becomes evidence of what you gave. Feeds stay, but they rank for the value they add to a life rather than the minutes they take from it. Working with human nature is cheaper and more durable than working against it.

### 2.6 Fractal dominoes

The six sources reinforce each other. A better story makes people look for collaborators. Better coordination produces visible progress. Visible progress feeds the story. A feed that surfaces solvable problems sends people toward the routing system, and a culture that admires giving funds the rest. Each item tips the others, which is why the checklist describes a domino effect rather than six separate projects.

The pattern is also fractal. It repeats at every scale. A person flourishes when their own story, relationships, imagination, attention, sense of worth and ability to create are healthy. So does a city, and so does a species.

### 2.7 Protopia

The goal is not utopia. Writer Kevin Kelly coined the word protopia for a world that is a little better today than yesterday, through steady and compounding improvement. Utopia promises perfection and dystopia predicts collapse, and both make people passive. Protopia is a direction rather than a destination, and anyone can move toward it this year.

## 3. The checklist

| # | Source | The infection | The flip | Beachhead |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Story | A story of decline: we are doomed, divided and powerless | Humanity as one superorganism learning to heal itself | A new story of protopia, told at scale |
| 2 | Coordination | The people who could solve a problem together never meet | Route everyone to the people who share their vision | The Social Router |
| 3 | Vision | The futures we picture most vividly are dystopias | Let anyone preview, visualize and back a future | The Vision Market |
| 4 | Attention | Feeds tuned for outrage keep billions in a victim state | Rank feeds by the value they add to each person | Bloomscroll |
| 5 | Status | Status has become the worship of money | Make giving the status symbol | Charity designer goods |
| 6 | Gatekeepers | A few platforms and funders decide what gets built | Let anyone co-create digital experiences | The Co-Creator Portal |

**1. Story.** People act out the story they believe. The sociologist Robert K. Merton named this the self-fulfilling prophecy in 1948. A species that expects collapse stops trying to prevent it. The flip is a story in which every problem is solvable and protopia is something we build together. This checklist is meant to be its first page.

**2. Coordination.** The Social Router is a coordinated social router, like an automated LinkedIn for visions of the future. You say what world you want to build, and it introduces you to the people, projects and resources that complete your piece.

**3. Vision.** We cannot build what we cannot see. The Vision Market is a market for visions of the future, where anyone can create, visualize and back a picture of where humanity could go. The strongest visions attract people, talent and money until a shared picture becomes a shared plan.

**4. Attention.** Social media is the nervous system of the superorganism. Bloomscroll is a feed that measures success by who you become, not how long you stay, and that shows people problems they can actually help solve. It moves the nervous system from a victim state into a problem-solving state.

**5. Status.** More than $300 billion a year goes to designer goods. Charity designer goods make the thing worth showing off the amount you gave, so the status economy starts funding the cure.

**6. Gatekeepers.** The Co-Creator Portal gives anyone shared tools to imagine, build and launch digital experiences together, moving creation from hierarchy to collective intelligence.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 How the sources were chosen

A candidate counts as a source only if it passes five tests.

1. **Shared:** it appears beneath many problems that otherwise look unrelated.
2. **Upstream:** changing it changes behavior before problems form, not after.
3. **Reach:** it is a system most people pass through every day.
4. **Flippable:** it can be redirected rather than eliminated.
5. **Buildable:** it admits a concrete first project that a small team can start.

Some tempting candidates fail. "Greed" fails the flippable test as stated; status is the form of it that can be redirected. "Lack of resources" fails the shared test in a world that spends more than $300 billion a year on designer goods; the resources exist. "Bad leaders" is real but sits downstream of the story people believe and the way they coordinate, and it offers no buildable beachhead for a small team.

### 4.2 How a beachhead is judged

A beachhead is the first foothold on a source. Good beachheads share six traits.

- **Focused:** it targets one source, even if it helps others.
- **Small to start:** a small team can launch it on a modest budget.
- **Compounding:** each new user, vision or connection makes it more valuable to the next.
- **Self-sustaining:** it lines up with incentives people already have, so it does not depend on charity forever.
- **Open:** it is open source or interoperable, so others can build on it.
- **Measurable early:** it has a leading indicator that moves within months.

### 4.3 Mapping symptoms to sources

The base of the triangle lists world problems and links each to the sources that most directly feed it. Climate change, for example, is linked to the story, coordination and vision. Mental health is linked to the story and attention. Every problem is linked to the story, because the story shapes all of them.

These links are hypotheses, not findings. They are published openly so that anyone can challenge or improve them through the project's repository.

### 4.4 Measuring progress

Systemic change is slow and hard to attribute, so the checklist relies on leading indicators that move first. These are proposals for each beachhead, not results.

| Source | Leading indicator |
|---|---|
| Story | Share of people who expect the future to be better, tracked in repeated surveys |
| Coordination | Introductions that turn into active collaborations; time from idea to team |
| Vision | Visions with real backers; people who can name a shared positive goal |
| Attention | How people feel after using the feed; share of time spent on things they can act on |
| Status | Money given through charity designer goods; share of luxury spending redirected |
| Gatekeepers | Projects co-created and launched by people without institutional backing |

Lagging indicators are the problems at the base. They should be watched, but read with care, because every one of them has many causes.

### 4.5 Revision

Like an aviation checklist, Humanity's Checklist carries a revision number. Rev. 1 is the first public version. Items can be sharpened, split or replaced as evidence comes in, and every change happens in the open.

## 5. The $5 million question

The checklist poses $5 million as a question rather than a promise: what if that was all it took?

The reasoning is about leverage. Money spent on a symptom scales roughly in a straight line: twice the money treats about twice the cases. Money spent on a source can scale far faster, because it changes systems that billions of people pass through every day. The beachheads are mostly digital and cultural, with low marginal cost. They are designed to compound through network effects. They are open, so others can build on them. Each needs a focused seed rather than a permanent budget.

For scale, $5 million is under nine minutes of the world's spending on designer goods, and a rounding error in a year of charitable giving. If the bet is wrong, the loss is small. If it is right, it changes how the world thinks about giving. That asymmetry is the case for trying.

## 6. Charitology

Humanity's Checklist grew out of Charitology, the study of charity. Charitology asks what giving actually changes, for whom, for how long and through which systems. Four principles carry over from it.

1. Give to sources as well as symptoms.
2. Judge giving by leverage, not only by cost per outcome.
3. Coordinate givers, because isolated budgets miss the connections between problems.
4. Make generosity visible and admired, because status steers money.

## 7. Objections and limits

**"This is too simple."** The list is short on purpose, because a checklist that cannot be remembered cannot be acted on. Each item is a hypothesis that can be tested and revised.

**"Relief still matters."** It does. Nothing here argues for abandoning people who need help today. The checklist is about where the next dollar can do the most, and it argues that some of that dollar belongs at the source.

**"Technology will not fix social problems."** Several beachheads are digital, which invites the charge of techno-solutionism. The targets, though, are cultural: what we believe, admire and pay attention to. Technology is the delivery mechanism, not the theory of change.

**"Who decides the story or the vision?"** Nobody alone. The Vision Market is plural by design, the Co-Creator Portal is open, and the checklist itself is open source and revisable.

**"Can $5 million really do this?"** Nobody knows yet. That is why it is posed as a question, and why each beachhead comes with indicators that can show early whether it is working.

**"Systemic effects are hard to prove."** They are. Attribution is the hardest part of systemic work, which is exactly why it is neglected. The answer is not to avoid it but to measure leading indicators honestly and publish the results.

## 8. Taking part

The checklist is open source. Its text, questions and argument live in a public repository, and anyone can propose changes. People can open an issue or a pull request, and AI agents can follow the instructions in the repository's AGENTS.md file. The project gets better as more people sharpen it.

## Glossary

- **Source:** a system that, in its current form, keeps producing many problems at once.
- **Symptom:** a problem experienced directly, such as hunger or loneliness.
- **Beachhead:** a focused first project that wins a foothold on a source.
- **The flip:** redirecting a source's energy so it produces repair instead of harm.
- **Protopia:** a world that gets a little better every year; Kevin Kelly's term.
- **Superorganism:** humanity viewed as one collective body with organs that can malfunction or heal.
- **Bloomscroll:** a feed ranked by the value it adds to each person; the opposite of doomscrolling.
- **Social Router:** a system that routes people to collaborators who share their vision.
- **Vision Market:** a market for visions of the future.
- **Charity designer goods:** desirable goods whose design shows how much the owner gave.
- **Co-Creator Portal:** shared tools for co-creating digital experiences without gatekeepers.
